Inside Shmirat Halashon

Cheshbon hanefesh applied to the mouth. Cheshvan has no yom tov to hide behind, which makes it the month to listen to the room. Cycle 3 reframes speech as kindness or harm — the joke at someone's expense, the defense nobody offered, the word that made a child want to come back tomorrow.

A lesson, as a teacher opens it

Tied to the week the school is on. No PDF, no email.

Cycle 4 · Week 2 · HAKARAT HATOV

The people who hold the door

9–1245 minutesPrep: Almost none

Students spend a week noticing the people in the building whose work is never announced — the custodian, the bus driver, the kitchen staff, the secretary who finds the lost coat. The lesson ends with each student writing one letter, by hand, to one of them.

BY THE END, STUDENTS CAN
  • Name three people whose work in the building usually goes unseen
  • Explain why hakarat hatov is an obligation rather than a courtesy
  • Write a thank-you that names something specific, not something general
YOU WILL NEED
  • Letter paper, one sheet per student
  • Envelopes
  • The building's staff list (ask the office)
THE LESSON
  1. The count5 min

    Ask: how many adults work in this building? Write the guesses on the board, then read the real number from the staff list. It is always higher than the guesses.

  2. Who did we miss?15 min

    In pairs, students list every adult they can name. Then read the staff list aloud. The gap between the two is the lesson.

  3. Specific, not general10 min

    Read two thank-you letters — one vague, one specific. Ask which one the reader would keep.

  4. Write one15 min

    Each student writes one letter to one person on the list. Collect them. Deliver them by hand, not by pigeonhole.

TO TALK ABOUT
  • “Is thanking someone for doing their job still hakarat hatov?”
  • “Who thanked you this week? Who did you thank?”
  • “Why does a handwritten letter land differently from a message?”
